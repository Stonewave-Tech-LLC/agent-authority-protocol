# AAP review, November 2026

- **Date:** 2026-11-12
- **Scope:**
  - the whole repository at `main` (`01236a2`);
  - the design drafts `docs/design/0001`–`0006`, committed with this review;
  - the results of the iOS trusted-surface spike S1;
  - the current landscape of agent-authorization specifications.
- **Status:** review and proposals. Nothing in this document is decided. Section 6.3 records where the maintainers stand.

## 1. Summary

AAP set out to answer one question: *can a third party check, without trusting the agent, that an AI agent may do X on behalf of a person or a company?* For the part the code base covers, it answers that question well. It has:

- a deterministic verifier;
- signed delegations that can only narrow;
- signed receipts for every decision;
- an RFC 9901 SD-JWT stack with Delegate SD-JWT chains;
- AP2-compatible constraints and receipts;
- an App Attest enrollment service.

At the review baseline, 236 tests passed.

Three things have changed since the October design drafts:

1. **The chain format is no longer a gap.**
   - Payments have AP2 and Mastercard's Verifiable Intent, both now at the FIDO Alliance.
   - For general mandates, at least four individual IETF drafts appeared in October and November 2026. They define signed, attenuating agent mandates with constraints and a validation procedure.
   - A small project will not win a format race against these, and it does not need to.
2. **The human-approval gap is still open.** All of those efforts either cover approval only for payments (AP2, Verifiable Intent) or explicitly leave out how the human consents (the IETF mandate drafts, WIMSE AIMS). Design drafts 0002 (Trusted Surface) and 0005 (use cases) cover exactly this gap.
3. **There is a concrete first use.** A product team using AAP needs provable human approvals for actions that agents take on behalf of a company.

The review also found one critical and four high-severity bugs. None of the affected code runs in production. All five are fixed, together with several lower-severity findings, in PRs #9–#12 (section 3).

**Proposal (not decided):**
- Position AAP as **an authority and approval-evidence layer under existing formats**, not as a format of its own.
- Freeze the v0 envelope except for security fixes.
- Let signed human approvals with passkeys be the first new capability.

**Where the maintainers stand (2026-11-12):**
- The scope is wider than human approval: AAP should make authority claims securely verifiable in large systems, between agent and human as well as between agent and agent.
- A concept comes before the next build milestone.

Details are in sections 6 and 7.

## 2. What AAP is today

### 2.1 Two stacks in one repository

| Stack | Crates | Format | State |
|---|---|---|---|
| **v0** | `aap-core`, `aap-verifier-service`, `aap-admin`, `aap-demo-cli`, `aap-wasm-demo` | Its own compact-JWS `Delegation` and `AgentMessage` envelopes, scope language and `Receipt` | Complete, hardened in PR #5; not in production |
| **v1** | `aap-sdjwt`, `aap-authority` | SD-JWT (RFC 9901), Delegate SD-JWT chains, AP2 open and closed mandates, Mandate Receipts | Library only (milestones M1–M3); no service exposes it |
| **Issuer** | `aap-issuer-service` | App Attest enrollment and assertion binding (R1) | Library and service on Postgres; not in production |

The two stacks share no code: `aap-authority` depends only on `aap-sdjwt`, and the services depend only on `aap-core`. Milestone M4 (v1 endpoints in the verifier service) has not started.

### 2.2 What the protocol guarantees

With correctly configured keys and a correct verifier, AAP v1 gives a verifier the following guarantees:

- **Attribution to a key.** The principal's key signed the root mandate, and the key named in the previous link's `cnf` signed each further link.
- **Only narrowing.** The closed mandate, the concrete action, must satisfy the constraints of *every* link in the chain, not just the last one (cumulative evaluation). A child link cannot widen what its parent allowed, even if the narrowing check at issuance has a bug.
- **Freshness and audience.** The final key binding is bound to this verifier (`aud`), to a nonce, and to a short time window measured on the verifier's clock.
- **Fail-closed evaluation.** Unknown constraint types are rejected, and so are undisclosed claims that could be restrictions (since PR #10).
- **A signed record of every decision.** Every accept and every reject produces a Mandate Receipt.
- **The typestate rule.** Code that grants authority cannot be handed an unverified token; such code does not compile.

### 2.3 What it does not guarantee (yet)

- **That a human approved.** A signature proves only that a key signed. With `aap-admin` (AL0) the key sits on the same machine as the agents. Design 0002 already notes that an agent with shell access can then approve its own pairing. The Trusted Surface (0002) is designed, and spike S1 measured it on a real iPhone, but it is not built.
- **What the human saw.** "What you see is what you sign" needs a deterministic renderer per constraint type. 0002 specifies that renderer as a rule, but no renderer exists.
- **Revocation in v1.** v0 has a status endpoint. v1 has no Token Status List yet (R3).
- **Budgets across verifiers.** Each verifier tracks cumulative budgets on its own. A usage ledger that spans verifiers is designed (0002, CG4) but not built.
- **Who the human is.** There is no identity binding yet; 0003 designs one.

## 3. Security and correctness findings

**Method.** There were four independent reviews: `aap-core` with the services, `aap-sdjwt`, `aap-authority`, and `aap-issuer-service`. Each finding below was reproduced, either by a test in a scratch crate outside the repository or by a regression test in one of the fix PRs that fails on `main`. Baseline: `cargo test --workspace` on `01236a2` gave 236 passed and 1 ignored.

### 3.1 The four October bugs

All four are fixed (PR #5, merged 2026-10-18) and covered by regression tests:

| Bug | Status | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Time windows used the agent's own timestamp | Fixed | `scope::matches` takes the verifier's `now` |
| Negative amounts passed the ceiling | Fixed; a related hole (B1 below) is fixed in PR #9 | `valid_amount` in `matches` and `narrows` |
| Message `issued_at` unchecked | Fixed | 300 s max age, 60 s skew; nonce pruning in the service |
| Sub-delegation could rewrite `principal_id` | Fixed; reuse of `delegation_id` (B4 below) is fixed in PR #12 | `verifier.rs` chain loop |

### 3.2 New findings, fixed

The severities rate the code as a library; none of it runs in production.

| # | Severity | Finding | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | **Critical** | **Undisclosed constraints failed open.** RFC 9901 lets the holder omit any concealed claim, and `aap-sdjwt` dropped unmatched digests without a trace. An agent that left out the disclosures of `amount_range`, `allowed_payees` and `budget` got a 9,999,999 EUR payment to anyone accepted. | PR #10 (merged): hidden claims are rejected unless hiding them can only narrow, such as allow-list members |
| A2 | High | **A re-encoded signature reset budget counters.** The ledger key hashed the whole JWT. ES256 is malleable (`s` → `n − s`), and high-S signatures cannot simply be refused, because the Secure Enclave produces them. | PR #11: the key is computed over the signing input |
| A3 | High (DoS) | **Unbounded nesting of disclosures.** About 280 kB of chained disclosures, signed by any agent with a delegated key, aborted a thread with a 2 MB stack. | PR #11: nesting depth limited to 64 |
| A5 | Medium | A past `execution_date` slipped under `not_after` after the window had closed. | PR #11, which also makes sure the leeway for a just-written date never extends past `not_after` |
| A7 | Low | An agent nonce could be replayed in the last second of its freshness window. | PR #11 |
| B1 | High | **A child could add a money ceiling its parent never granted.** `narrows` compared amounts only when the parent had a ceiling, and only the leaf scope was matched. | PR #9 (merged), with cumulative matching over every link |
| B2 | High | **Out-of-range minutes stretched a time window.** `00:00-00:1439` passed `narrows` against a one-hour parent and matched the whole day. | PR #9 (merged): strict `HH:MM` |
| B3 | Medium | Weak Ed25519 keys were accepted under non-strict verification. With the identity point as the agent key, one signature verified for every message. | PR #12 |
| B4 | Medium | A child could take over another delegation's `delegation_id`, either its parent's or any other registered delegation's, and so inherit that delegation's revocation status. | PR #12: ids are unique within a chain, and in the service a status row only applies to the token it was stored for |
| B6 | Low | No cap on chain length, and the service queried the database once per link before any signature check. | PR #12: 8 links, checked before any parsing or database work |

The review recorded further medium- and low-severity findings in the v1 library, the v0 service and the issuer service. They are tracked privately, and this document will list them once they are fixed.

**The issuer service** follows Apple's App Attest verification steps 1–9 correctly:
- the certificate chain to the pinned root;
- the nonce extension;
- `rpIdHash`;
- the counter, with atomic counter updates;
- the AAGUID policy;
- the key id.

Challenges are single-use, the SQL is parameterized, and no secrets are committed.

### 3.3 Code quality and test coverage

The code is small, readable and well commented, and individual checks fail closed. The October fixes are correct. `aap-sdjwt` has unusually thorough negative tests for the format rules (31 in `negative.rs`) and byte-exact RFC 9901 vectors.

The weaknesses sat at the *seams*:
- between SD-JWT and policy (A1);
- between a mandate's identity and its signature (A2);
- between `narrows` and `matches` (B1, B2).

Tests mostly proved that honest inputs work and that documented attacks fail. They rarely generated adversarial inputs:

- `narrows` had no tests for a missing parent ceiling or for malformed times.
- No test had the holder pick disclosures adversarially.
- The property tests used the code under test as their own oracle.
- The fuzz targets are built but never run, and they cannot get past the signature check.

Recommended standing rule: **every security property gets a property test with an independent oracle, and every service gets a "before the signature check" test.** Two examples of such properties:
- `narrows(p,c) ∧ matches(c,r) ⇒ matches(p,r)`;
- "any subset of disclosures is accepted only if the full set is".

### What this means for AAP

- **Already covered by bigger players, so AAP should not compete there:**
  - payment mandates (AP2, Verifiable Intent);
  - agent and bot identity (Web Bot Auth, TAP, A2A signed cards, WIMSE);
  - enterprise employee-to-tool access (MCP EMA with ID-JAG);
  - the format for generic attenuating chains (several IETF drafts that will converge in a working group).
- **Still open:**
  1. **Provable human approval of a specific non-payment action on a trusted device**, producing evidence a third party can check. Verifiable Intent does this for payments only, the IETF drafts leave it out on purpose, and FIDO has published nothing yet.
  2. **A reference verifier with signed receipts** for non-payment authority. The drafts define formats and validation steps, but few ship code.
  3. **A small, self-hostable issuer** for individuals and small firms. Okta and Entra serve enterprises that run their own identity provider.
  4. **Binding a verified identity** (the EUDI wallet, or a passport chip as a fallback) to a mandate. Nobody defines it yet, and the wallets only arrive from December 2026 onward.
- **Risk:** FIDO's Agentic Authentication group may publish a trusted-surface or passkey-approval profile within 6–12 months. AAP should be designed so that it can adopt that profile, not compete with it.

## 5. Where AAP fits

### 5.1 In the world

A standalone standard or a new token format is not a promising direction for AAP. FIDO, the IETF and the card networks are all in that race now. Design 0001 already drew the same conclusion for payments.

The proposal is to treat AAP as **a narrow layer: authority checks and approval evidence for agents that act outside the system that runs them**. It would serve the product teams that use it first, and be an open, credible reference implementation second. Concretely:

- **Keep:** the deterministic verifier, cumulative evaluation, the fail-closed rules, Mandate Receipts, and the typestate discipline. The drafts describe these parts but rarely implement them.
- **Adopt** other people's formats wherever they exist:
  - SD-JWT and Delegate SD-JWT (done);
  - AP2 mandates for payments (done, as interop);
  - later, whatever the IETF mandate drafts converge on for general authority.

  AAP constraint types should map onto RAR (RFC 9396) `authorization_details` where possible.
- **Own** the part nobody else does: the approval step. That means three things:
  - a canonical rendering of the action;
  - a signature by a key the human controls (passkey first, a dedicated issuer app later);
  - a signed approval record that a verifier can check without trusting the agent or the platform.

### 5.2 Where a cryptographic proof adds something

Within a single platform, a database record of a decision is often enough. Signed evidence adds value where a claim has to be trusted by someone who does not share that platform's database or trust its operator:

| Situation | Does signed evidence add value? |
|---|---|
| A human approves an action an agent will take outside the company, such as a message, an offer or a payment | **Yes.** It proves *which human* approved *exactly this*, in a way that survives outside the platform, and it holds even if the platform, an admin or an automation is compromised. |
| Actions that may never run without a human | **Yes.** This is AAP's per-action (closed) mandate at a higher assurance level. |
| An agent runtime on a person's own device executes work sent from the cloud | **Yes.** The device is a separate trust domain and should check the delegation, not just the connection. |
| Delegation between agents inside one runtime and one database | **Usually not.** Keep the *rule* (narrowing only) and AAP's semantics, and enforce them by policy. Sign chains once agents run in different trust domains. |
| An agent of one company deals with an agent of another | **Yes, in the long run.** It is the case the IETF mandate drafts target, and AAP should stay compatible with them. |
| Recipients of agent-sent messages ("did a human at that company approve this?") | Possibly later, through a verifiable approval reference in the message. |
| Login and identity | **No.** AAP does not replace a login. |

### 6.2 Proposals on what to drop or defer (not decided)

- **Drop:** new work on the v0 envelope (frozen), and extending AP2 payment constraints beyond interop tests. AP2 and Verifiable Intent cover payments.
- **Keep as a designed fallback, but do not build now:** the passport-chip Verified Human Credential (0003). The maintainers see it as a possible universal fallback next to the EUDI wallet. Building it needs an HSM, a data-protection review and liability handling.
- **Defer:** the transparency log (R4), Android Key Attestation, and the relay and push service (R5). They are good designs that have no user yet.
- **Open: the dedicated iOS Issuer App.**
  - **For passkeys:** they give a hardware-backed, biometric-gated signature on every platform today.
  - **Their weakness:** the screen that shows the action belongs to the requesting app, so a compromised front end could show one thing and ask the passkey to sign another.
  - **What the app adds:** a separate issuer app with its own renderer closes that gap. It becomes worth building once an action's value justifies it.
- **Retire:** the v0 verifier service and device pairing, once nothing depends on them.

## 7. Notes on the design drafts

The drafts 0001–0006 are committed with this review as *Draft*. The only changes are wording that marks working positions as not decided, plus the removal of references to non-public systems. They hold up well. In particular, 0002's threat model, 0004's cumulative evaluation and 0005's use-case catalogue are better than most of what the new IETF drafts say about consent.

Corrections from this review:

- **0001:** the landscape table dates from 2026-10-18. Section 4 above supersedes it, especially for Verifiable Intent, the IETF mandate drafts and WIMSE AIMS.
- **0001, open question 3** (payments first or non-payment first): this review proposes non-payment first. The question stays open until the concept is done.
- **0002:** passkeys deserve a full assurance level (AL2-P), not a footnote. They are the fastest route to a human-held key, and the route WIMSE AIMS recommends. Section 6.2 states the trade-off against the Issuer App.
- **0004:** milestones M0–M3 are done. Proposal: redefine M4 as W1 above, not as a generic v1 endpoint.
- **0006:** R1 is done. R2 (the Attested User Key credential) is only needed for the Issuer App path. Proposal: move it to "Later" unless the Issuer App is chosen.
